Status and scope
This is the Step 3 deliverable from the build prompt: a STRIDE threat model of the design
in Step 1, the Section 10 control mapping with evidence and tests, and a list of what the
design cannot fully meet. No code is written until you approve this doc and Step 2.
It assesses the MVP (Phases 1 to 3) in depth, because real quality records go live at Gate
2. Phase 4 to 7 items (Teams, SharePoint, DocuSign, mobile) are covered at the threat level
only and get a deeper pass before their phase starts.
Inputs it relies on:
Step 1 plan as revised on 12 Nov 2026, build prompt Revision 2.2, and your answers in
the open questions sheet.
Your answers today: code lives in a GitHub repository, Steps 2 and 3 come before any
code, and you (Victoria) are the named pull-request reviewer.
Working assumptions still open: RPO 1 hour, RTO 1 business day, 99.5% availability, 25
concurrent users, 60-minute leaver target.
Ratings use a simple scale: likelihood and impact each Low, Medium or High. They are my
judgement for a 22-user internal system on Azure, not measured figures. Annex A
references follow ISO/IEC 27001:2022 as I understand the numbering; check each against
your licensed copy.
System and trust boundaries
The API is the only component that changes records, so most controls sit where requests
cross into it. Workers make every outbound call, which keeps a failing integration away
from users.

What is checked at each crossing:
B1, device to app: TLS 1.2+, WAF and rate limits, then token validation, user status and
permissions in the API.
B2, Entra to app: signed OIDC tokens (issuer, audience, tenant, auth_time, acrs);
SCIM calls need a secret token.
B3, app to data: private endpoints only; the app's database role cannot update or
delete signatures or audit entries.
B4, app to anchor: workers can add anchor files but nothing can change them until
the WORM policy expires.
B5, app to third parties: outbound only, credentials from Key Vault; inbound
webhooks (DocuSign, Phase 6) come back through B1 and are HMAC-checked.

Abuse cases specific to an eQMS
These six scenarios are the ones an ISO auditor or pen tester is most likely to probe. Each
becomes a scripted test before Gate 2.
1. Forged approval. Someone signs a policy as their manager using an unlocked laptop.
Stopped by: every signing action forces a fresh Entra sign-in with MFA (T02). The
open session alone cannot sign.
Left over: if the manager's MFA device is also unlocked and to hand, the system
cannot tell. That is a device and behaviour control, covered by your ISMS policies.
2. Rewritten history. After an audit finding, someone edits an old approval date or
deletes a rejection.
Stopped by: insert-only grants on signatures and audit entries (T04), the hash
chain and WORM anchors (T07), hourly and nightly verification.
Left over: a cloud super-admin edit inside the 15-minute anchor window (T08).
3. Admin approves their own change. The System Administrator adds themselves to the
Approver role, then approves a document.
Stopped by: SoD guard if they authored it (T11); privilege-change alert; quarterly
access review.
Left over: with about 22 users there may be only one admin, so nobody
independent sees the alert in time (T12).
4. Leaver keeps working. A leaver keeps using an open browser tab for an hour after HR
offboards them.
Stopped by: per-request status check and revoke-now (T24). SCIM alone takes a
provisioning cycle (about 40 minutes reported; verify).
Left over: the 60-minute target relies on someone pressing revoke-now for urgent
cases.
5. Insider takes the ISMS pack. A user bulk-exports the risk register and Statement of
Applicability before leaving.
Stopped by: classification limits on export, watermarked downloads with the
viewer's name, export logging, and a mass-export alert.
Left over: a permitted user can still read and photograph what they are cleared to
see.
6. Backdoor in the code. A pull request from the AI agent or a compromised
dependency adds a hidden bypass to the signing check.
